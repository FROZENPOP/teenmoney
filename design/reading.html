Your first paycheck: six things to know

Example text. The real reading comes from the measure task and covers the same six ideas as the quiz.

When you get a job, the hourly rate times your hours is your gross pay. Work 60 hours at $12.00 an hour and your gross pay is $720.00. Your paycheck will be smaller than that, because taxes come out first: federal income tax, Social Security and Medicare. What lands in your account is your take-home pay, sometimes called net pay. Plan with that number, not the bigger one.

Once you know your take-home pay, split it. Needs are what you can't skip: getting to work, eating, keeping your phone on if your job needs it. Wants are everything else, like concerts, new shoes or another streaming service. Wants are fine. Pay for needs first, then decide how much of what's left goes to wants and how much to savings.

Read your bank statement every week. Most lines will be things you remember buying. Look for the ones you don't: a free trial that turned into a monthly charge, a fee you didn't agree to, or the same purchase charged twice. If you find one, call the bank or the company. You can often get it back.

An emergency fund is money you keep for surprises, like a cracked phone or a bike repair. It isn't for things that go on sale. Even a small fund means a surprise doesn't push you into borrowing.

"Buy now, pay later" splits a price into smaller payments. It can look cheaper, but late fees and interest can make the total higher than paying once, and the payments keep coming after the excitement is gone. Add up every payment before you say yes.

When your income drops, act early. Look at what's still coming up, cut wants before needs, and see if you can earn a little more, like picking up a shift. Borrowing to cover the gap costs extra, so it should be the last step, not the first.
